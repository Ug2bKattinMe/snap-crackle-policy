How to read it

  • Forecasts are modeled estimates, not official projections. Each shows a median and a 95% interval from thousands of simulated scenarios; the interval doesn't cover new legislation, court rulings or a recession.
  • Each person is counted once. People who lose more than one kind of help, or lose a job and coverage, aren't double counted.
  • Hover any map or chart for exact figures, and open each tab's methods and sources for where every number comes from.

How these are counted

  • "Using the program" means enrolled. Enrollment counts people with coverage, whether or not they used care.
  • Medicare only is total Medicare enrollment minus all dual enrollees. Medicaid or CHIP only is Medicaid and CHIP enrollment minus full-benefit dual enrollees, because CMS's Medicaid count includes only people with full Medicaid benefits and leaves out partial-benefit duals.
  • Dual enrollees are from CMS Medicare enrollment data for early 2026, as published by KFF. Medicare Advantage is from CMS's October 2026 contract, plan, state and county report and includes PACE and cost plans; plan counts of 10 or fewer in a county are suppressed in that file and counted as zero.
  • The years differ. Total Medicare by state is 2024 (the latest year KFF publishes), Medicaid and CHIP is July 2026, and population is the Census Bureau's July 2025 estimate. Because Medicare enrollment has grown since 2024, the Medicare-only share is likely a little low.
  • Puerto Rico and other territories are left out. Alaska and Hawaii are moved and resized to fit.
  • Each map's color scale runs from its own lowest to highest state, so compare shades within a map, not across maps.
  • SNAP overlap is a model, not a count. It starts from CBPP's state estimates of adults 55–64 and parents of school-age children newly subject to SNAP work rules (FY2022 data), then applies uncertain national rates: 30–48% of those parents have a youngest child 14 or older; 18–50% of people newly subject lose SNAP (Arkansas's 2018 Medicaid experience to CBO's estimate); 65–85% of older adults and 35–65% of parents in expansion states are expansion adults also facing Medicaid work rules (0–10% in non-expansion states); 15–40% of the children in those households leave SNAP with the parent; and 40–55% of dual enrollees are on SNAP, of whom 1–5% lose it. Each range is a stated assumption, not a published rate.

2027 coverage, SNAP and jobs impact forecast

By the end of June 2027, about — people are forecast to have lost Medicaid, SNAP, Medicare or Marketplace help, or a job tied to the cuts, counting each person once. By the end of 2027 that rises to — (95% interval — to —), with about — in lost economic output over the year.

Updated November 22, 2026: after the midterms

The forecast now includes four changes since the election. Democrats won majorities in both the House and the Senate, but the new Congress takes office January 3, 2027, after the rules start, and changing the law still needs the president's signature or a veto override.

  • CMS delayed enforcement. On November 13, CMS let states hold off on removing anyone for not meeting work requirements until June 30, 2027. States still check work status from January and decide by December 11 whether to take the delay. California (4.7 million adults facing the rules), New York, Maryland, Connecticut, Kentucky and the District of Columbia have said they will; Louisiana is seeking a grace period, and Nebraska is already enforcing. The forecast assumes states covering 60% to 95% of expansion adults take it, so far fewer people lose Medicaid by June and fewer by December.
  • A court widened the medically frail exemption. A federal judge blocked CMS's narrower definition, so people with a qualifying condition don't have to prove they can't work. The forecast trims work-rule losses slightly.
  • The 2027 Part B premium is final. CMS set it at $209.40 a month, up $6.50, replacing the projection.
  • SNAP kept falling. USDA data now run through August 2026: 35.8 million people, down 5.6 million from a year earlier. Both models now start from that larger drop, which raises the SNAP estimate.

Still open: a December 11 government funding deadline, Democrats' plans to restore enhanced Marketplace tax credits and reverse Medicaid cuts in 2027, and each state's choice on the delay. The 95% intervals do not include possible new legislation. The October jobs report (165,000 jobs, 4.1% unemployment) is in line with the Fed outlook the forecast already uses.

Methodology

This is a scenario forecast built for this page, not an official projection. Every input range is listed below with its basis.

  1. Start from the people each 2027 rule reaches. Medicaid expansion adults by state (20.4 million, scaled to CMS's June 2025 count), SNAP recipients (FY2025, USDA), immigrants losing Medicaid or Medicare (CBO), Marketplace enrollees and Medicare enrollees, all from the companion enrollment page and 2027 policy report.
  2. Apply loss rates and timing. Each group gets a range for how many lose coverage or benefits and how fast. Medicaid losses build through 2027 as states check work hours and run six-month renewals; SNAP losses are already in place from the November 2025 rule change; Marketplace losses land mostly at the January 1 plan-year start.
  3. Add job losses. Jobs lost from the cuts equal lost federal dollars times jobs per $1 billion from an input-output model (Commonwealth Fund/GWU, IMPLAN). The economy-wide change is the Federal Reserve's unemployment projection applied to the labor force. The larger of the two is counted.
  4. Remove double counting. Adults who lose both Medicaid and SNAP under the same work rules are counted once, as are job losers already in another group.
  5. Turn people into dollars. Federal spending reduction = people losing each program times its average federal cost (Medicaid per expansion adult, SNAP benefit, premium tax credit), over the months they are off. GDP loss applies a spending multiplier to each. State and local tax loss is a share of GDP loss. For hospital and ER care, the people who lose Medicaid, Medicare or Marketplace coverage and stay uninsured are multiplied by ER visit and hospital stay rates and by the hospital cost of each visit and stay (HCUP); the unpaid share of that cost is uncompensated care. New out-of-pocket costs are applied per newly uninsured person-month. Medicare cost increases apply to everyone who pays the higher Part B premium and Part D costs.
  6. Simulate 20,000 scenarios. Each scenario draws every input from its range (uniform or triangular). The headline is the median scenario; the 95% interval is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile. States get each scenario's national totals in proportion to their enrollment, SNAP, population or Medicare shares, so state intervals move together.
  7. Report at 6 and 12 months. People counts are as of June 30 and December 31, 2027; dollars are totals over January–June and January–December 2027, in 2027 dollars.

How the coupled model works
  1. A monthly chain for the 20.4 million expansion adults. Each person is in one of 48 combined states: Medicaid, uninsured or other coverage; never on SNAP, on SNAP, recently cut or cut; employed or not; subject to work rules or exempt.
  2. Checks drive Medicaid losses. Each person's first check comes at their next annual renewal, then every six months. Failure rates are set from Nebraska's 2026 results, are higher for adults not employed and for people already cut from SNAP, and lower for exempt adults. People can re-enroll after showing a compliant month. A second run without the new rules is subtracted.
  3. SNAP losses are anchored to the fall observed since July 2025, with the share caused by the law as a range, plus further 2027 losses. Inside the chain, adults not working lose SNAP under the time limit.
  4. Losing SNAP raises the odds of a hospital stay on a logistic scale: an odds ratio for losing participation times an odds ratio per $10 of benefit, converted from 2026 prices to the study's prices, building up over three months.
  5. Jobs and coverage are linked. Job loss and finding rates follow the unemployment path; a hospital stay can lead to job loss; not working raises the chance of failing the Medicaid check; in non-expansion states a job loss can push low-income Marketplace enrollees below 100% of poverty.
  6. Hospital care for the newly uninsured uses ER and stay rates relative to insured adults from 2023–2026 payer data, at 2027 hospital costs; part of the unpaid cost may pass to commercial premiums.
  7. Inputs move together. One shared factor links Medicaid, SNAP and Marketplace losses (state paperwork and verification), another links unemployment, job loss and hiring. 10,000 scenarios; 95% intervals are the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles.

What makes South Carolina different

  • No Medicaid expansion. Parents qualify only up to 67% of the poverty line and most other adults not at all, so about — adults fall in the coverage gap (2024 estimate). A state request to cover up to — working parents, with its own work requirement ("Palmetto Pathways"), is still awaiting federal approval and is not in the forecast.
  • SNAP is falling faster than the nation. Recipients went from — to — in a year. The forecast counts 45% to 85% of that drop as caused by the 2025 law.
  • The Marketplace already took a hit. Coverage fell —% in early 2026, and approved 2027 rates rise —% on average before subsidies. The forecast counts only the further 2027 losses from new verification and immigrant rules.
  • After the election. Governor-elect Alan Wilson won on November 3 and said during the campaign that Medicaid expansion could crowd out other state priorities, so the coverage gap is likely to remain. The Palmetto Pathways request is still pending with federal officials.
  • The state budget is exposed. South Carolina's SNAP error rate was —% in FY2025. Getting it under 6% before the FY2028 cost share is set would avoid the benefit bill entirely.
How the South Carolina forecast works
  1. SNAP starts from South Carolina's own drop of — recipients (July 2025 to July 2026), with the share caused by the law between 45% and 85%, plus further 2027 losses at South Carolina's share of national SNAP.
  2. Marketplace takes national 2027 losses at South Carolina's share of 2026 Marketplace enrollment (about 2%), not its population share. The 2026 drop is context, not counted.
  3. Immigrant rules reach South Carolina at its share of national Medicaid and Medicare enrollment. No Medicaid work requirement or six-month renewals apply.
  4. Jobs, the economy and hospitals use the same multipliers, rates and 2027 costs as the national models, applied to South Carolina's federal dollars and newly uninsured.
  5. State costs add the SNAP administrative share (50% to 75%, about $23 million a year) and, from October 2027, the benefit share by error-rate tier.
  6. 20,000 scenarios; 95% intervals are the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles. Counties share the state's scenarios, so their intervals move together.
